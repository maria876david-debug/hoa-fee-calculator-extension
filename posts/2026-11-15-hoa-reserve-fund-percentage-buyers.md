# What a Healthy HOA Reserve Fund Percentage Actually Tells a Buyer

Two homes can charge the same monthly dues and be in completely different financial shape. The difference usually sits in one number most buyers never ask about: the reserve fund's percent funded.

**What the percentage means.** A reserve study estimates what the association will need for big-ticket repairs — roofs, elevators, paving, boilers — and how much it should already have saved. The percent funded compares the money actually in the reserve account against that target. It is the single clearest snapshot of whether today's dues are honestly covering tomorrow's repairs.

**The thresholds that matter.** A score of 70% or higher is considered healthy. Anything below 30% indicates severe underfunding — and, as the buyer protection guide on HOAFeeCalculator.com puts it, that virtually guarantees upcoming special assessments. Between those poles, the trend matters as much as the number: a fund climbing toward 70% is a different risk from one sliding toward 30%.

**Lenders look at it too.** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines require at least 10% of the association's annual operating budget to be allocated directly to capital replacement reserves. A community that fails basic financial-health tests can make the whole development non-warrantable, which means your loan — not just the seller's problem — can be rejected.

**How to check before you commit.** State law generally gives buyers a statutory review window of about 3 to 10 calendar days. Put the latest full reserve study at the top of your document list, ahead of meeting minutes and insurance certificates, and check the percent funded ratio first. A low fee paired with a starved reserve fund is not a bargain; it is a deferred bill with your name on it.
